F1–F12, rethought in the notation

Writing the recognizer changed what the design question even is. Five options, compared in the language rather than in prose.

The short version

Building the parser settled the chord's safety question — an empty body is structurally distinct from a non-empty one, so the lock toggle can never collide with the F-layer and needs no timeout. But it also made the alternation (space|capslock) visible on the page, and once you can see it you have to ask why it is there: the two triggers are redundant, and that redundancy is an entire unused layer. Spending it is cheaper than the chord and needs no state-machine surgery at all.

1What building the recognizer taught us

1.1 — The empty body is the lock's signature, and it is structural

These two are different shapes, not different timings:

GestureBodyMeaning
(capslock+space).emptyToggle the CLX lock
(capslock+space)[ 1 ]one tapF1 — the proposal

Every key in a body fires on its own key-down, so by the time the closing bracket arrives, "was the body empty?" is already answered. No hold threshold, no clock, no guessing at intent — which is exactly where the existing 200 ms Space timeout goes wrong. The chord design is safe by construction, not by tuning.

1.2 — Release order is noise, and the engine currently listens to it

The recognizer normalises every group to + because which finger lifts first is not something a person controls. The engine should hold the same view — but today it does not quite: clx_up acts only when trigger == Some(code), and trigger_key is nulled by the first release. Behaviour therefore depends on release order. It happens to be benign now; it is fragile the moment a second gesture depends on the same edges.

1.3 — The letter space is already full

A tempting option the notation surfaces is a sub-layer: clx[ f[ 1 ] ] — hold a trigger, hold a layer key, strike a digit. The body can contain a hold, so the language expresses it fine. The keyboard does not:

1.4 — (space|capslock) is a wasted layer

Writing the alternation down is what exposed it. The two triggers are interchangeable everywhere: space[ h ] and capslock[ h ] do the identical thing, and is_trigger_key treats them as one. That redundancy is not free — it is a whole second layer of the entire keyboard, sitting unused, and it costs no new key to spend.

1.5 — Correction: the left hand is not pinned

I claimed the chord strands F1–F5 on the wrong hand. That is wrong, and the counter-example is a posture I had not considered: pinky and ring finger take the whole left column — CapsLock, Shift, Ctrl are stacked there — the thumb takes Space, and because nothing is anchored on home row the middle and index fingers roam the number row freely, reaching 1–5 without the right hand moving at all.

So the reach objection to design B is withdrawn, and B's score improves from third to a close second. The same posture is what makes design D comfortable too — D just asks for fewer fingers.

Why this wins

  • Two keys, not three. Rollover stops being a blocker — no three-non-modifier chord, so M3 evaporates.
  • No engine surgery. No deferred lock, no chord detection, no 200 ms race to fix, no chord_active plumbing. The engine already records which trigger is held; the module just has to read it.
  • Nothing is given up. Alt+Space stays, the desktop bindings stay, the lock gesture stays, and all four modifiers stay free to decorate the F-key.
  • It spends a resource that was already being wasted rather than borrowing one that is in use.
  • CLX is out of keyspace — every letter A–Z is claimed (§1.3). D is the only option on the table that manufactures room rather than consuming the last of it.

With the reach objection withdrawn, B is genuinely close. The trade is now clean: B pays once in engine work (and gets the L4 and T2 bug fixes bundled in) to preserve the mental model; D pays forever in a learned asymmetry to get a two-key gesture and near-zero implementation risk. I still lean D on the keyspace argument, but this is a preference call, not a technical one.

What it costs, honestly

Trigger symmetry. Today the two triggers are interchangeable and you never think about which one you grabbed. Afterwards, the number row means two different things depending on that choice. Everything else stays symmetric, so the asymmetry is confined to one row — but it is a real thing to learn, and it is the whole price of the design.

capslock[ 3 ] stops switching desktop. If that is in your fingers, this will bite for a week.

Locked mode has no trigger held, so active_trigger is None and a bare digit has to pick a layer. Keep the status quo — desktops — which means the F-row is unavailable while locked.

If you disable CapsLock as a trigger, the layer disappears. Prefs should say so.

5Open questions

Q1Is losing trigger symmetry acceptable? It is the entire price of D.Yes — the redundancy buys a layer, and the asymmetry is confined to the number row. But it is your muscle memory, so it is your call.
Q2Which trigger gets the F-row?CapsLock. Space is the one you hold while typing, so leaving the more disruptive layer on the less-used trigger is safer.
